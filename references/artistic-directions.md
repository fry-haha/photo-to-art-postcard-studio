# Confirmed artistic directions

Read this reference when the request is to turn a real photograph into collectible art, an art print, or the visual master for an art postcard. Do not load it for ordinary border, sticker, or production-only work.

## Shared transformation contract

The outcome is a personal memory transformed into an art object, not a tourist souvenir or a generic filter demonstration.

Before rendering, identify and lock:

- primary subject and its full recognizable silhouette;
- faces, hands, clothing, signs, landmark geometry, object construction, horizon, and important edges;
- original crop intent and natural color relationships;
- source facts that may appear as text: place, date, subject name, or a user-supplied note.

Across all three directions:

- keep the source photograph recognizable at first glance;
- do not invent, delete, duplicate, relocate, or redesign people, buildings, objects, weather, or scenery;
- do not change identity, ethnicity, expression, body, clothing, pose, or meaningful accessories;
- keep readable source text accurate or remove it;
- preserve the source palette unless the direction calls for controlled tonal separation; never apply an unrelated preset;
- use texture as a material layer, not as permission to redraw the scene;
- generate the artwork master before postcard layout;
- reject any result that reads primarily as an AI illustration.

## Output contract

- The source photograph is input evidence, not an output style.
- The system has exactly three artistic outputs: `D1 Photo + Oil Fusion`, `D2 Artist Travel Archive`, and `D3 Museum Art Collection`.
- An ordinary production request returns the selected direction only. A three-direction comparison returns exactly one result for each of D1, D2, and D3.
- A comparison may display the untouched source separately and label it `REFERENCE ONLY`; never count or style it as a fourth version.
- Do not place all three results inside one forced postcard-shaped canvas. Each result inherits the source-led composition decision below.
- When these artworks become postcard fronts, each one requires a direction-matched back. The back is not a fourth artistic direction; it is the paired functional side of D1, D2, or D3.

### Postcard completion gate

A postcard set is complete only when:

- every D1, D2, or D3 front has one corresponding back;
- front and back have identical pixel dimensions, aspect ratio, and orientation;
- the back contains usable writing space, an address treatment, and a stamp position unless the user explicitly requests a collector-only art back;
- the back carries the front's material language without repeating the full artwork or becoming a generic postal template;
- supplied lyrics, dates, places, or credits are exact; otherwise use no invented facts and only intentional placeholders such as `PLACE` or `TIME` when a reusable template needs them;
- one proof sheet or index makes every front/back pairing unambiguous.

## Composition intelligence

Composition is selected from the photograph, not from a reusable merchandise template. The method library below is a vocabulary for decisions, not a checklist. Use the fewest methods that clearly support the subject—normally one structural method, one depth or balance method, and optionally one light, color, or rhythm method.

### 1. Subject analysis

Before rendering, record a compact `composition brief`:

1. **Subject class:** landscape, architecture, portrait, object, performance, street scene, or mixed scene.
2. **Primary subject:** the element the eye must recognize first.
3. **Secondary evidence:** contextual elements required for place, story, scale, or memory.
4. **Protected region:** the smallest complete envelope containing every area that must not be cropped or redrawn.
5. **Identity-critical detail:** face, hands, garment, signage, landmark profile, roof, spire, instrument mechanism, artwork, or other defining feature.
6. **Dominant axis:** horizontal, vertical, diagonal, radial, curved, or no strong axis.
7. **Eye-flow cues:** gaze, movement, road, shoreline, railing, shadow, light beam, repeated columns, or contour direction.
8. **Depth structure:** flat plane, foreground/middle/background layers, corridor, aerial depth, or isolated subject.
9. **Visual weight:** the position and strength of bright, dark, saturated, detailed, human, and text-bearing areas.
10. **Usable negative space:** genuinely quiet space that can remain empty or host D2 notes; it is not permission to cover meaningful background.
11. **Natural ratio:** the source ratio and the ratio that best preserves its dominant axis and protected region.
12. **Edge risks:** any head, hand, horizon, building, tree, object, or line already close to an edge.

If primary subject, protected region, or natural ratio cannot be identified confidently, preserve the original ratio and crop exactly.

### 2. Aspect-ratio rules

- Preserve the native aspect ratio by default. Change it only when a candidate ratio creates a demonstrably stronger hierarchy without damaging the protected region.
- Horizontal landscapes normally remain horizontal: use the native ratio or a nearby `4:3`, `3:2`, `16:9`, or `2:1` family according to the breadth of the scene. Use panorama only when the subject genuinely unfolds laterally.
- Architecture follows the building, not the product. Use horizontal framing for a façade or ensemble; vertical framing for towers, interiors, stairwells, columns, or strong upward emphasis; preserve a wide ratio for long rooflines or arcades.
- Portraits may use `3:4`, `4:5`, or `2:3` when the body or vertical environment supports it. Keep a horizontal portrait when gaze, travel context, or environmental negative space is essential.
- Objects may use square or near-square framing only when the silhouette is compact or radial. Long instruments, tables, vehicles, and tools follow their dominant axis.
- Do not convert an image to vertical merely because a future postcard could be vertical.
- For a D1/D2/D3 comparison, keep the same ratio family unless D2 needs a small outward paper extension. Compare artistic direction, not three unrelated crops.
- Prefer expanding quiet background or adding an external paper field over trimming a protected subject. Any generated extension must remain non-narrative and cannot invent new objects or architecture.

### 3. Crop decision rules

Apply this order:

1. Lock the protected region and identity-critical details.
2. Test the native crop first.
3. Identify only genuine distractions or dead space; do not call contextual scenery a distraction merely because it is complex.
4. Test no more than three conservative crop candidates drawn from the natural ratio family.
5. Reject any candidate that clips the protected region, weakens the dominant axis, breaks a leading line before it reaches the subject, or removes necessary scale/context.
6. Prefer the candidate that improves focal hierarchy with the least lost source information. If improvement is marginal, retain the original crop.

Hard prohibitions:

- Do not cut through a head, chin, face, hand, joint, instrument mechanism, meaningful accessory, readable sign, complete landmark silhouette, roof peak, spire, arch, mountain ridge, or horizon merely to fit a ratio.
- Do not crop a building into an arbitrary detail when the building itself is the subject. A detail crop is valid only when that detail was identified as the primary subject.
- Do not place a portrait face against the edge it is looking toward; preserve gaze or movement room.
- Do not create accidental tangencies where a subject edge barely touches the frame.
- Do not use generative filling to rebuild a missing part of a person, landmark, or object. Expand only quiet, non-factual background when necessary.

### 4. Composition selection procedure

1. Build the composition brief.
2. Choose two to five candidate methods from the library whose activation signals are present in the source.
3. Select one **primary structure method** and at most two **supporting methods**. Do not apply methods merely to reach a quota.
4. Reject a method if it requires relocating, duplicating, inventing, or heavily cropping source content.
5. Rank remaining candidates by: subject integrity first, natural-ratio fit second, focal hierarchy third, eye flow and depth fourth, direction compatibility fifth.
6. Keep the native composition when no candidate creates a clear improvement.
7. Record the decision as: `ratio / primary method / supporting method(s) / protected region / crop action`.

### 5. Composition method library

The following 65 methods are available. Their activation notes define when they are useful.

#### Placement and balance

1. **Rule of thirds:** place an already off-center subject or horizon near a third when it improves balance without forced cropping.
2. **Golden ratio grid:** use for unequal but calm distribution of a dominant mass and supporting space.
3. **Golden spiral:** use when existing curves or nested shapes naturally lead inward to the focal point.
4. **Center composition:** use for a singular commanding subject, frontal portrait, radial object, or monumental façade.
5. **Axial symmetry:** use when architecture, reflections, corridors, or paired forms are genuinely symmetrical.
6. **Near symmetry:** retain small irregularities when a mostly symmetrical scene feels more human or historically real.
7. **Asymmetrical balance:** balance an off-center subject with negative space, light, color, or a smaller secondary element.
8. **Dynamic balance:** use unequal masses when diagonal movement or tension is part of the memory.
9. **Counterbalance:** let a small bright, saturated, human, or detailed element answer a larger quieter mass.
10. **Triangular visual-weight balance:** connect three existing focal weights to stabilize a complex scene.

#### Lines, shapes, and geometry

11. **Leading lines:** use roads, rails, shores, beams, or architecture only when they point toward the primary subject.
12. **Converging lines:** use corridors, streets, ceilings, or façades to intensify depth around a real vanishing point.
13. **S-curve:** use rivers, paths, bodies, shorelines, or cables that already guide the eye through multiple depths.
14. **C-curve:** use a single embracing curve to contain a subject or open toward negative space.
15. **Diagonal composition:** use slopes, gestures, shadows, or architecture for energy and directional movement.
16. **Triangular composition:** use three people, peaks, objects, or structural points that already form a stable triangle.
17. **Pyramidal composition:** use a central rising subject whose broad base and apex create monumentality.
18. **Radial composition:** use domes, wheels, flowers, records, or rays that organize around a center.
19. **Circular or oval enclosure:** use when a real circular form contains or echoes the focal subject.
20. **L-shaped composition:** use an existing strong vertical plus horizontal plane to shelter an open field.
21. **Z-shaped eye path:** use alternating diagonals across layered landscapes or interiors when the path remains legible.
22. **V-shaped composition:** use valleys, receding walls, arms, or converging forms that cradle the focal point.
23. **Repetition of verticals:** use columns, trees, windows, or people to create cadence and upward emphasis.
24. **Repetition of horizontals:** use waterlines, shelves, steps, or strata to create calm and breadth.
25. **Geometric abstraction:** use only when actual architectural or object geometry is the subject; do not deform it into invented shapes.

#### Space, depth, and viewpoint

26. **Foreground–middle-ground–background layering:** use when all three planes contribute scale and immersion.
27. **Overlapping planes:** use natural overlaps to show depth while keeping subject boundaries readable.
28. **Atmospheric perspective:** use haze, decreasing contrast, or cooler distance already present in landscapes.
29. **Linear perspective:** preserve straight structural recession in streets, rooms, bridges, and façades.
30. **Single vanishing point:** use tunnels, corridors, roads, or central interiors with one dominant destination.
31. **Multiple vanishing points:** use corner buildings or wide interiors only when their geometry remains accurate.
32. **Scale progression:** use repeated objects or people of decreasing size to reinforce depth.
33. **Occlusion depth:** use partial overlap when it clarifies spatial order; never hide identity-critical detail.
34. **Frame within a frame:** use real windows, doors, arches, branches, or openings that already surround the subject.
35. **Layered framing:** use foreground and architectural frames together only when neither becomes clutter.
36. **Foreground anchor:** keep one meaningful near element to prevent a landscape or interior from floating.
37. **Deep-focus composition:** use when relationships across the full scene are important.
38. **Shallow-focus isolation:** use when a portrait or object is primary and the blurred context still communicates place.
39. **Low-angle monumentality:** use for architecture or people when the upward view is source-authentic and complete.
40. **High-angle overview:** use for tables, streets, interiors, or collections when spatial organization is the subject.
41. **Eye-level intimacy:** use for portraits and personal objects when direct human-scale connection matters.

#### Frame, ratio, and negative space

42. **Edge-to-edge authority:** use when the photograph already has strong internal composition and needs no paper field.
43. **Fill the frame:** use for a compact subject or meaningful detail when background adds no story and protected edges remain safe.
44. **Environmental context:** use when landscape, room, street, or travel setting is part of the memory.
45. **Active negative space:** use quiet space to support gaze, movement, atmosphere, or D2 annotation.
46. **Breathing-room margin:** use a modest empty field around a delicate object, portrait, or archival study.
47. **Look room:** preserve space in front of a face, animal, vehicle, or directional object.
48. **Panoramic composition:** use for continuous lateral terrain, architecture, processions, or long interiors.
49. **Vertical emphasis:** use for towers, trees, standing figures, falling water, columns, or upward light.
50. **Square containment:** use for radial, centered, or compact still-life subjects—not as a universal social format.
51. **Wide establishing view:** use when place and spatial relationships are essential evidence.
52. **Tight detail view:** use only when the detail has independent narrative or material value.
53. **Reflection split:** allow a centered division when a true reflection creates meaningful bilateral structure.
54. **High horizon:** use when foreground texture, water, land, or human activity carries the story.
55. **Low horizon:** use when sky, ceiling, light, or vertical atmosphere is dominant.

#### Rhythm, light, color, and emphasis

56. **Pattern repetition:** use repeated windows, waves, seats, trees, or objects to create rhythm.
57. **Pattern interruption:** focus on a real deviation in an existing pattern; never invent the exception.
58. **Visual rhythm:** alternate scale, spacing, light, or form to guide scanning through the image.
59. **Color-weight balance:** balance strong source colors by their visual mass, not by recoloring the scene.
60. **Complementary contrast:** emphasize an existing complementary pair when it naturally separates subject and setting.
61. **Analogous harmony:** use closely related source colors for a quiet, unified atmosphere.
62. **Single accent color:** let one existing saturated element carry focus against quieter surroundings.
63. **Tonal-mass balance:** organize large light, middle, and dark areas so no accidental corner dominates.
64. **Light-path composition:** follow existing illumination from source to focal area; do not manufacture a new beam.
65. **Chiaroscuro emphasis:** use real high-contrast light and shadow for drama while retaining shadow detail and factual lighting.

### 6. When to use which methods

| Source analysis | Strong starting candidates | Avoid by default |
|---|---|---|
| Wide lake, sea, plain, or mountain range | horizontal thirds, high/low horizon, panorama, atmospheric perspective, foreground layering, light path | vertical template, arbitrary center crop, cutting a ridge or shoreline |
| Single mountain, tree, island, or waterfall | center or thirds, pyramidal/vertical emphasis, scale progression, negative space | equal texture everywhere, cropping the apex or base |
| Frontal façade or formal interior | axial or near symmetry, center, vertical rhythm, one-point perspective, tonal-mass balance | casual asymmetry that bends structure, clipping roof/spires |
| Corner building, street, stair, arcade | diagonals, converging lines, multiple vanishing points, L/Z shape, foreground anchor | flattening perspective, severing leading lines |
| Environmental portrait | thirds or asymmetrical balance, look room, shallow-focus isolation, environmental context, eye-level intimacy | forced vertical crop, removing meaningful setting, clipping head/hands |
| Formal or close portrait | center or near symmetry, eye-level intimacy, breathing room, tonal balance | notes over the face, texture over eyes/teeth, edge pressure around gaze |
| Compact object or still life | center, square containment, triangular balance, fill frame, texture contrast | generic wide landscape layout, cutting functional parts |
| Long object or instrument | follow dominant axis, diagonal or horizontal rhythm, negative space, mechanism protection | square crop that cuts the ends, invented hardware |
| Repeating architecture or collection | pattern repetition, pattern interruption, rhythm, deep focus, color-weight balance | random collage, equal emphasis on every element |
| D2 annotation-led source | active negative space, breathing margin, asymmetry, frame-within-frame | covering evidence, adding a paper field by cropping the subject |

Direction-specific composition behavior:

- **D1** keeps the photographic crop strongest. Use the selected lines, light path, depth, and focal hierarchy to place brush texture; do not let brushwork become a new composition unrelated to the source.
- **D2** places notes and sketches in existing negative space first. If space is insufficient, extend a paper field outward while keeping the photographic protected region intact; do not shrink the photograph into a generic centered card.
- **D3** may use the most decisive crop and tonal hierarchy, but only after the protected-region gate passes. Museum restraint never justifies cutting a landmark, head, hand, or defining object part.

Research grounding: Nikon's composition guidance emphasizes subject identification, horizon choice, leading lines, patterns, and justified center composition; Adobe's guidance emphasizes balance, negative space, depth, leading lines, and fill-the-frame only when background is not narratively useful. These principles inform the selection logic above rather than acting as mandatory formulas.

## Direction 1 — Photo + Oil Fusion

### Core concept

A real photograph remains structurally photographic while painterly light, oil-brush movement, canvas tooth, and layered pigment atmosphere become visibly present. It should feel like the photograph has been physically preserved inside a painting surface.

### Artistic intention

Raise an ordinary personal photograph into an emotionally rich art print without losing the factual memory recorded by the camera.

### What should change

- Add visible canvas tooth and selective oil-brush texture.
- Translate broad light fields into layered pigment atmosphere.
- Let large quiet areas such as sky, water, walls, or blurred background carry the strongest brushwork.
- Use medium brush movement to reinforce existing contours, light direction, and depth.
- Allow slight tonal consolidation where it strengthens hierarchy.

### What must remain unchanged

- Subject identity, silhouette, geometry, pose, proportions, and source perspective.
- Facial features, eyes, teeth, hands, landmark edges, instrument construction, and meaningful text.
- The underlying location, objects, weather, and natural color relationships.
- No new clouds, windows, people, plants, props, reflections, or scenic elements.

### Composition rules

- Preserve the source composition unless a deliberate crop materially strengthens the focal hierarchy.
- Never crop through a face, complete landmark, instrument mechanism, mountain ridge, or horizon accidentally.
- Keep crisp photographic anchors in the focal subject; let brushwork increase away from those anchors.
- Use one dominant light path and one clear focal area. Do not distribute equal painterly emphasis everywhere.

### Color and lighting rules

- Start from the source colors; deepen or layer them rather than replacing them.
- Painterly light must follow the photograph's real illumination direction.
- Permit localized warm/cool pigment interplay, but keep skin, landmark materials, and signature object colors believable.
- Avoid blanket sepia, teal-orange grading, uniform desaturation, or artificial sunset conversion.

### Texture and material rules

- One canvas surface plus one oil-brush hierarchy is sufficient.
- Strongest texture: sky, water, wall, foliage mass, or background atmosphere.
- Medium texture: clothing, ground, and secondary architecture.
- Minimal texture: faces, eyes, teeth, hands, fine signage, architectural seams, strings, and mechanisms.
- Texture must reveal the photograph beneath it; reject opaque impasto that replaces detail.

### Typography rules

- Typography is optional at the artwork stage.
- If used, set only a confirmed place or artwork title plus an optional small medium label.
- Prefer a restrained serif or quiet humanist sans; one display line and one metadata line maximum.
- Do not imitate painted lettering or place type across a face or focal edge.

### Select this direction when

- light, weather, color atmosphere, or surface emotion is the main value;
- the source has clear silhouettes and enough resolution to survive texture;
- the user wants a recognizable signature effect with stronger artistic transformation;
- landscapes, architecture, music objects, studios, concerts, and environmental portraits need emotional depth.

### Common failures

- Full AI-painting conversion that changes the subject.
- Thick, uniform brush texture over faces and details.
- A color preset mistaken for oil fusion.
- Invented clouds, reflections, windows, fingers, strings, or ornaments.
- Flattened depth because every area receives equal texture.
- Portrait beautification or loss of facial identity.

## Direction 2 — Artist Travel Archive

### Core concept

The real photograph becomes the central evidence inside an artist's field study: observation notes, source-derived sketches, paper history, registration marks, and restrained archival indexing record how the memory was seen.

### Artistic intention

Make a private journey feel observed, collected, and authored. The result should resemble a working travel study with emotional intimacy, not decorative scrapbooking.

### What should change

- Integrate a warm or neutral archival paper surface.
- Add a small number of handwritten observations derived from known facts or visible conditions.
- Add graphite, ink, or dry-brush sketches derived only from real contours in the photograph.
- Introduce restrained registration crosses, field numbers, date marks, or measurement cues where useful.
- Allow selected photo edges to dissolve into paper while keeping the subject intact.

### What must remain unchanged

- The photograph remains the dominant evidence, not a background decoration.
- Subject identity, landmark geometry, object structure, source palette, and scene facts remain true.
- Do not invent diary events, coordinates, dates, weather notes, or personal quotations.
- Never sketch over a face, important hand action, readable sign, or defining object mechanism.

### Composition rules

- Use asymmetry and active negative space; avoid a centered scrapbook grid.
- Keep roughly 65–85% of the visual information photographic, 10–25% notes/sketches, and the remainder paper breathing room.
- Attach annotations to specific visible evidence through proximity or a fine leader line.
- Use one main annotation zone and no more than two supporting sketch zones.
- Preserve complete subjects; dissolve only nonessential outer atmosphere into paper.

### Color and lighting rules

- Preserve the photo's color identity, allowing paper warmth to influence only highlights and margins.
- Sketches should use graphite, umber, charcoal, muted ink, or one source-derived accent.
- Do not recolor the photograph to match the paper.
- Maintain the source light direction and local contrast around the primary subject.

### Texture and material rules

- Choose one paper family: field-notebook stock, cotton rag, aged archive, or dry watercolor paper.
- Add only restrained wear: faint fibers, one fold, edge handling, or registration marks.
- Sketch lines must feel physically drawn, with pressure variation and imperfect endings.
- Avoid default scrapbook tape, stickers, stamps, torn collage piles, dense stains, and fake antique damage. When tape or another banded material is explicitly requested or justified by the input, select it through `visual-system.md`; keep it optional, source-grounded, and tied to a named spatial task.

### Typography rules

- Handwriting carries observations; a small serif or monospace may carry catalog facts.
- Use only confirmed place, date, time, subject, or user-provided wording.
- Prefer fragments such as light, material, weather, route, or observed shape rather than invented prose.
- Keep type secondary to the photograph and visually distinct from handwritten marks.

### Select this direction when

- the memory benefits from personal authorship, context, or story;
- travel, architecture, craft, music practice, studio objects, or location studies contain observable details;
- the user wants the most intimate and personal-brand-friendly direction;
- a consistent series can reuse annotation grammar while changing the notes for each source.

### Common failures

- Generic scrapbook collage or travel-journal clip art.
- Invented dates, coordinates, weather, quotations, or personal history.
- Notes unrelated to visible evidence.
- Too much paper coverage, making the photograph secondary.
- Sketches crossing faces or redrawing the subject inaccurately.
- Repeating identical notes and marks across every image.
- Decorative pseudo-handwriting that is unreadable and meaningless.

## Direction 3 — Museum Art Collection

### Core concept

A real personal photograph is treated with the restraint, hierarchy, tonal depth, and catalog discipline of a gallery photography print or museum collection image.

### Artistic intention

Make the memory feel selected, permanent, and collectible through curation rather than decoration.

### What should change

- Strengthen focal hierarchy through controlled crop, tonal separation, local contrast, and quiet negative space.
- Refine the image surface toward a museum photography print: matte depth, rich blacks, clean highlights, and subtle archival tooth.
- Introduce minimal accession-style metadata only when it supports the collection concept.
- Remove visual noise only through crop or tonal restraint; do not delete factual scene content.

### What must remain unchanged

- Subject identity, geometry, real objects, location, expression, materials, and source facts.
- The artwork stays photographic; no simulated historical painting or fantasy reconstruction.
- No arbitrary antique color cast, ornate frame, gallery wall mockup, or souvenir emblem.

### Composition rules

- Use one decisive crop and one focal hierarchy.
- Protect complete landmarks, heads, hands, instruments, and meaningful scene relationships.
- Prefer edge-to-edge image authority or a deliberate field of negative space; avoid decorative borders inside the artwork.
- Remove competing emphasis through tonal control, not blur pasted over important content.

### Color and lighting rules

- Preserve natural color while improving tonal separation.
- Deepen blacks without crushing detail; control highlights without flattening luminous areas.
- Allow a restrained warm or cool print bias only when already supported by the source.
- Do not manufacture dramatic light, golden hour, spotlighting, or storm conditions.

### Texture and material rules

- Use fine matte photo-paper tooth, cotton-rag surface, or subtle archival pigment-print character.
- Texture is nearly invisible at normal viewing size and evident only on close inspection.
- Avoid heavy canvas, obvious brushwork, scratches, stains, torn edges, or distressed overlays.

### Typography rules

- Typography is sparse, exact, and curatorial.
- Use one place/title line and, optionally, one accession or medium line.
- Prefer refined serif, neutral grotesk, or restrained monospace metadata; no more than two type personalities.
- Keep typography outside faces, complete landmarks, and other focal content.

### Select this direction when

- the source already has strong composition and needs elevation rather than overt transformation;
- architecture, minimal landscapes, still life, portraits, performances, or music objects need premium commercial consistency;
- a series must feel coherent across varied subjects;
- the user wants the most scalable direction for a repeatable AI Skill.

### Common failures

- Merely darkening the photograph and calling it museum quality.
- Tourist-postcard typography, centered souvenir labels, or decorative frames.
- Excessive sepia or fake historical aging.
- Empty minimalism that removes the memory's emotional specificity.
- Crushed shadows, muddy skin, clipped highlights, or over-sharpened edges.
- Accession text that invents institutional ownership or factual metadata.

## Direction selection

Use the user's stated choice when available. Otherwise score the source against the following criteria and choose the strongest match:

| Source need | Photo + Oil Fusion | Artist Travel Archive | Museum Art Collection |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| Light and atmosphere are the main emotion | High | Medium | Medium |
| Personal notes and remembered context matter | Low | High | Medium |
| Strong existing composition needs restraint | Medium | Low | High |
| Music, craft, or studio identity | High | High | High |
| Maximum series consistency | Medium | Medium | High |
| Maximum intimate personal-memory feeling | Medium | High | Medium |

If two directions score equally, generate one controlled comparison before committing. Keep crop and source facts locked so the comparison tests the direction rather than a different photograph.

## Prompt contract

For any direction, construct the image prompt with these fields:

1. `Edit target`: identify the supplied source photo.
2. `Direction`: use one confirmed direction name exactly.
3. `Locked invariants`: subject identity, geometry, crop-critical edges, real objects, text, and original color relationships.
4. `Allowed transformation`: list only the direction-specific light, texture, paper, notes, sketches, tonal, or typographic changes.
5. `Composition`: state focal area, protected edges, and where transformation may be strongest.
6. `Text`: quote only confirmed wording, or explicitly request no text.
7. `Avoid`: invented objects, fantasy, full illustration conversion, unrelated color grading, generic template graphics, logos, and watermark.

## Validation gate

Reject or revise the artwork when any answer is no:

- Is the original memory recognizable immediately?
- Are faces, landmarks, objects, and meaningful text still accurate?
- Is the chosen direction visible through more than a color filter?
- Is transformation concentrated where it supports hierarchy?
- Are all notes and metadata grounded in confirmed facts?
- Does the result feel like an art object rather than a template?
- Could the same decision rules be repeated on another source without copying the same layout?
