# Visual system

## Signature direction

Preserve the source as photography. Build the recognizable house style with film black/white, restrained extracted accents, irregular cuts, generous negative space, and a single analog texture chosen for the subject.

## Film-black and film-white grounds

Do not default to digital `#000000` and `#FFFFFF`. Select one pair per collection.

| Pair | Film black | Film white | Character |
|---|---|---|---|
| Neutral archive | `#171717` | `#F4F1E8` | balanced charcoal and warm paper |
| Cool cinema | `#111317` | `#F1F2EE` | blue-black and cool projection white |
| Warm negative | `#1B1714` | `#F3EBDD` | brown-black and aged cream |
| Silver gelatin | `#202124` | `#EEECE6` | darkroom gray and fiber paper |
| Soft contact sheet | `#24221F` | `#F7F3EA` | softened black and clean warm stock |
| High-contrast editorial | `#0E0E0F` | `#FAF8F1` | near-black and bright print white |

Use the photograph's emotional temperature to select warm or cool. Extract 3–6 accent colors from the supplied visual reference. If no reference exists, extract them from the source photograph. Do not automatically lower saturation.

## Product-color postcard backs

A postcard back is a designed product surface, not automatically an ivory form. Select a **hero ground color** from one semantically meaningful and visually identifiable region of the source. Good anchors include sea, sky, floorboards, lamp or stage light, façade stone, clothing, glass, vegetation, or a dominant object surface. Do not average the whole photograph into a gray-beige wash.

### Color decision

1. Identify the source region that carries the clearest memory or product identity.
2. Sample its representative hue while preserving the source's warm/cool character. Do not automatically desaturate it.
3. Choose one of three uses: a full-field ground; a color-dominant split in which the hue occupies at least about 60% of the back; or a tinted material field whose texture remains subordinate to the hue.
4. Set postal ink by contrast, not habit. On dark or mid-value blue/brown grounds, use warm white such as `#F7F1E6`. On light blue, yellow, cream, or stone grounds, use charcoal such as `#171717` or another source-derived dark ink.
5. Check the design at thumbnail size. The hue should be immediately legible, the postal structure should remain readable, and the back should visibly belong to the front.

Do not reduce every back to beige merely because the direction is archival or museum-like. Controlled chroma can still feel premium when spacing, materials, and typography remain exact. Avoid low-contrast tone-on-tone postal lines and cheap souvenir saturation.

### Subject pairings

- Coastal landscape: sea-derived light blue, with charcoal or deep blue-gray ink.
- Historical gallery or art space: floor-derived warm brown, with warm-white lines and type.
- Vinyl or music object: lamp-derived warm yellow or amber, with dark brown/charcoal ink.
- Lake or sky-led landscape: water/sky blue or blue-white, with deep blue-gray ink.
- Architecture: façade, stone, concrete, brick, glass, or metal color from the building itself.
- Portrait: a distinctive clothing color, provided it does not compete with the face; otherwise choose a strong environmental color tied to the person.

### Product-appeal gate

Reject a back that is technically elegant but visually inert. It must pass all four checks: a recognizable hero hue or deliberate black/white reason; clear postal contrast; a source-specific abstract element; and front/back collection unity. Color alone is not enough, but lack of a visible color decision is a failure when the source offers one.

## Texture library

Choose one main treatment and, at most, one subtle supporting treatment. Keep faces and important text clean.

1. Fine 35 mm grain — even micrograin, low opacity; good universal default.
2. Coarse high-ISO grain — visible clumps; useful for nightlife, concerts, and dark scenes.
3. Silver-gelatin fiber — soft paper tooth and luminous monochrome; good for portraits.
4. Matte photo-paper tooth — dry, restrained surface; good for travel and architecture.
5. Glossy photo-print sheen — controlled highlights and deep blacks; use only in mockups.
6. Contact-sheet dust — sparse dust, frame marks, and edge numbers; good for documentary sets.
7. Darkroom light leak — restrained warm or red edge exposure; use only when it supports the image.
8. Photocopy toner — rough black breakup and edge loss; good for punk or fan material.
9. Halftone print — visible dot screen; apply to secondary fragments, not the main face.
10. Risograph misregistration — limited-color separation and slight offset; optional secondary treatment.
11. Newsprint fiber — soft absorbent dots and gray paper; good for editorial collage.
12. Scanned paper crease — one or two folds, corner wear, or scanner shadow; preview use only unless requested.
13. Polaroid emulsion softness — gentle bloom and creamy highlights; useful for intimate memories.
14. Screen-print ink — opaque color and slight edge drag; good for bold graphic accents.

Avoid stacking grain, halftone, photocopy damage, light leaks, folds, and paper fibers at once. Texture should support the photograph rather than announce itself.

## Sticker edges

- Mix irregular handmade white borders with borderless cutouts when the composition benefits.
- Use free, slightly uneven contours rather than a uniform machine stroke for handmade borders.
- Keep the edge intentional and smooth enough to manufacture.
- Use shadows only in presentation mockups.

## Decorative elements

Allow a few stars, lines, tape strips, stamps, date boxes, frames, or abstract shapes. Derive their colors from the collection palette. Do not add false travel, celebrity, or personal details. Tape follows the material system below and is not a generic decoration.

## Tape and banded-material system

### Gate: optional, sourced, functional

Tape is an optional spatial element, not a required house signature. Before adding it, answer all three questions:

1. **Source:** what visible or semantic evidence in the current input supports this material, color, transparency, weave, pattern, or direction?
2. **Task:** what precise relationship changes because the band exists?
3. **Necessity:** would removing it weaken attachment, hierarchy, motion, concealment, edge crossing, or the idea of incomplete memory?

If any answer is vague, do not use tape. A previous successful strip proves only that its placement logic worked for that image; never copy its exact color, shape, angle, or location as a favorite device.

For photographs, derive evidence from clothing patterns, walls, paper, glass, rain, reflections, signs, light, water, vegetation, stone, record surfaces, or another real material. For music, text, or object-led inputs, derive it from rhythm, emotional cadence, surface, construction, and structural information specific to that input. Never invent a material merely to fill empty space.

### Distinct material families

Treat each family as physically different. Do not render all of them as the same semi-transparent office-tape rectangle.

| Material | Visual evidence and behavior | Best tasks | Avoid |
|---|---|---|---|
| Paper masking tape | matte, fibrous, slightly opaque, quiet torn or cut ends | fix a note, label, or image corner; mask minor information | plastic shine, perfectly repeated tears |
| Washi | thin paper, source-derived print or fibers, softer color transmission | connect archive fragments; carry a clothing/wall pattern | decorative pattern unrelated to source |
| Ribbon | woven or satin direction, soft highlight, flexible but not adhesive | continue movement, tie title to subject, form a long controlled barrier | torn-paper ends, random short tabs |
| Fabric band | visible weave, stitch direction, occasional restrained fray | extend clothing/material evidence, link portrait and label | generic beige burlap on every portrait |
| Vellum | milky diffusion, paper tooth, softens what lies beneath | veil secondary information, bridge photo and writing field | behaving like clear glossy plastic |
| Acetate | clear, crisp, slightly reflective, may refract or tint an edge | protect or frame an archive label, cross a photo edge, connect layers | cloudy wrinkles or paper fibers |
| Transparent film | nearly clear flexible surface, restrained bubble/crease/lift | memory interruption, reflection logic, long spatial connection | uniformly crumpled plastic or loud glare |

### Spatial tasks and length

Every piece must perform at least one task:

- fix a title, note, artwork label, photo echo, or correspondence panel;
- pass behind text, wrap one corner, or become a controlled underline while keeping primary copy legible;
- connect text to a real image feature or connect two layers;
- extend a seam, line, reflection, route, gesture, record groove, or other movement;
- mask secondary information without hiding source truth;
- cross the trim or image edge to create a deliberate inside/outside relationship;
- form a visual barrier;
- express memory loss, interruption, or partial recovery.

Let task determine length. A short strip fixes a local corner or nearby relationship. A long strip connects title and subject, continues motion, or establishes a barrier. Never leave a short strip stranded on a boundary or in blank space.

### Placement and angle: 20-method reserve

Angle is an outcome of geometry, not a decorative preset. Before placing a strip, mark connection point `A`, connection point `B`, protected regions, the nearest relevant edge, and the dominant source axis. Select one method below, calculate or visually follow its direction, then add only a small material-true imperfection. When no method creates a clear relationship, remove the strip.

1. **Edge-normal corner hinge** — cross one photo, sketch, or label edge at roughly 80–100° so the strip visibly fastens it; keep both ends supported by material.
2. **Corner-bisector clamp** — cross a single meaningful corner at roughly 35–55°; use only when both sides of the corner remain visible.
3. **Dominant-axis parallel** — run within about 0–5° of a real horizon, seam, groove, shelf, railing, or textile weave to extend continuity.
4. **Counter-axis stabilizer** — sit roughly 75–105° against a strong diagonal or vertical movement to hold it in place without cancelling it.
5. **Curve-tangent continuation** — follow the local tangent of a groove, orbit, hair curve, shoreline, cable, or arch rather than cutting across the curve arbitrarily.
6. **Radial anchor** — point toward the true center of a record, lens, wheel, lamp, rose window, or other radial source.
7. **Leading-line continuation** — extend a road, perspective ray, water course, gaze, gesture, tonearm, or drawn leader line through the material.
8. **Vanishing-point alignment** — align with one authentic perspective family in architecture or interiors; never introduce a competing false vanishing point.
9. **Structural-grid alignment** — follow columns, ceiling bays, façade modules, shelves, or registration axes with restrained near-horizontal or near-vertical placement.
10. **Type-baseline underline** — sit within about 0–3° of a title or caption baseline, beginning under meaningful words rather than floating beyond the copy.
11. **Type-corner wrap** — cross only one corner of a label or title block at roughly 10–25°, preserving every primary letter.
12. **Sketch-edge hinge** — cross the actual boundary between a source-derived sketch and open paper at roughly 65–95° so the tape visibly attaches the study.
13. **Photo-to-paper seam bridge** — span both sides of a photographic transition at roughly 70–110° to prove that two layers are being connected.
14. **Trim-edge overrun** — cross the physical card or image edge with enough length on both sides to create inside/outside space; avoid a short stranded tab.
15. **Point-to-point connector** — set the strip direction from the vector between two real elements, such as title-to-subject, label-to-silhouette, or groove-to-stylus.
16. **Weight-counterbalance diagonal** — use a shallow 6–18° counter-angle to balance a heavy visual mass; the strip must touch a real anchor rather than occupy empty space.
17. **Gesture-or-gaze echo** — follow a person's gaze, shoulder, garment seam, or movement while remaining outside the face, head, and narrative hands.
18. **Material-grain alignment** — follow visible fabric warp, wood grain, masonry course, rain streak, water ripple, acetate reflection, or paper fiber direction.
19. **Nonparallel paired attachment** — when two pieces are genuinely needed, give them different anchors and a 12–35° directional difference; never create equal parallel office strips.
20. **Near-orthogonal archive mount** — use a calm horizontal or vertical mount with only 1–4° physical irregularity when the layout is already archival and geometric; do not add a dramatic diagonal merely to look handmade.

### Angle-selection order

Use this order: real movement/structure (3–9, 17–18) → actual layer boundary (1–2, 12–14) → connection between two elements (15) → typography (10–11) → visual counterbalance (16 or 20). Method 19 is reserved for a justified two-piece construction. The chosen strip must touch, cross, wrap, underline, or continue the named anchor. A strip placed near an element without changing its relationship is still stranded.

### Variation, material state, and layering

When using more than one transparent piece, vary length, width, angle, starting point, opacity, overlap, and surface state. Do not create equal-height, equal-width, parallel strips. Preserve only a little wrinkling, bubbling, fray, or lifted edge where it explains real material and depth. Keep it irregular and localized; uniform distress is synthetic and distracting.

### Multi-piece tape ensembles

One piece is not a default limit. Use a 2–4-piece ensemble when the relationships genuinely require a small material composition. Assign roles before placement:

- **primary connector** links the source-derived abstraction to the postal or title structure;
- **secondary anchor** fixes a different edge, label, sketch, or material layer;
- **optional bridge** crosses a seam, continues movement, or carries the eye toward the stamp/address area;
- **optional accent restraint** underlines or masks a minor detail without becoming filler.

The ensemble needs one shared material story or palette, while individual pieces vary in material family, length, width, angle, opacity, attachment point, and surface state. Pieces must connect to actual elements, not merely connect to one another. Prefer structures such as a clamp pair, bridge-plus-anchor, one opaque piece plus one transparent piece, an edge relay, or a title-to-subject chain.

Avoid an unexplained X-cross, several strips fanning from one empty point, equal parallel tabs, scattered tape confetti, or a cluster whose only purpose is to look handmade. Remove one piece at a time during audit: if removing it does not change attachment, hierarchy, movement, concealment, or spatial depth, delete it. If the whole cluster can be copied unchanged to another postcard, redesign it from the source.

When a strip passes a person, building, or core object, establish an explicit front/behind relationship, transparent relationship, or material reason. Protect faces, heads, hands with narrative value, landmark silhouettes, rooflines, structural joints, readable signs, primary titles, stamp positions, and address lines. Never let a strip casually sever them.

When material interacts with type, it may pass behind the letters, wrap one corner, underline, or link copy to a real feature. Primary text must remain readable. If a strip can be moved anywhere without changing the composition, or copied unchanged to another work, redesign or delete it.

### Success patterns, not templates

- A short paper strip that visibly fixes a field sketch is evidence for **attachment**, not a reusable upper-left placement.
- A long transparent band following a horizon, reflection, seam, or record groove is evidence for **continuity**, not a universal translucent stripe.
- A vellum block that softens secondary detail behind a writing field is evidence for **hierarchy**, not a default pale rectangle.
- A fabric band continuing a real garment weave is evidence for **source grounding**, not permission to add textile to every portrait.
- A film crossing a trim edge can evidence **inside/outside space** or **memory interruption**, but it must not become a repeated signature.

### Mandatory failure check

Reject or redesign:

- **generic office tape** — one universal beige/clear rectangle regardless of source;
- **stranded tape** — a short piece isolated at a border or in empty space;
- **repeated favorite device** — repeating a previously successful strip by habit;
- **interchangeable overlay** — a band that can move to another work unchanged;
- **uniform strips** — equal length, width, angle, opacity, or spacing;
- **overworked material** — excessive, even, or attention-seeking wrinkles, bubbles, fray, gloss, or tears;
- tape that steals focus from the subject;
- unjustified crossing of a person, face, landmark, building, or core object;
- tape used only to fill blank space.

Final audit each piece for material, source evidence, connection points, direction, length, scale, overlap, surface state, protected regions, and named visual task.

## Landscape sticker language

Landscape sets may be more interpretive than object-based sets while remaining photographic. Prefer two to five distinct mechanisms rather than repeating the same rectangular crop:

- framed view or “window” into the scene;
- borderless complete silhouette for a landmark or tree cluster;
- vertical film slice that preserves the subject contour;
- close texture crop of water, bark, stone, cloud, or foliage;
- one graphic shape derived from a real contour in the source;
- one restrained map, route, stamp, or index element.

Do not cut mountain ridges, horizons, roofs, or major branches at arbitrary horizontal edges. If a crop crosses them, the surrounding frame must make the crop unmistakably intentional.

## Border-only comparison rule

When the request is to compare borders, keep the photograph itself identical in every sample: same pixels, crop, color, saturation, exposure, and orientation. Change only the white edge, film rebate, paper mount, tag, or other outer frame. Do not reinterpret the photo as halftone, risograph, cyanotype, photocopy, negative, paper relief, or another medium unless the user separately requests that experiment.

## Era-specific fan material

When a song/album/tour era is named, build a mini style lock before layout:

- period-accurate source photograph;
- two dominant era colors plus one source-derived accent;
- one stage, cover, video, wardrobe, or production cue verified from that era;
- only confirmed titles or user-supplied text;
- unchanged real face and body when using a photographic cutout.

Do not mix older hair, clothing, stage visuals, or album symbolism into a period-specific set unless the user asks for a career-spanning collage.
