# Crop and frame library

Use this reference before building portrait-detail stickers, architectural stickers, or film frames.

## Element-first rule

Treat the source as an inventory, not as a rectangle. Extract meaningful people, products, props, discs, packaging pieces, and small objects before designing frames. Preserve their real contours whenever the source supports a clean mask. Use a geometric frame only when the original image is intrinsically a complete rectangular scene, a real film frame, or a deliberate window view.

Reject a proposed sticker when its only distinction is that the same full image has been placed in a different container. An arch belongs to an arched view or arch-shaped subject; a circle belongs to a disc, lens, round prop, or intentionally circular crop; a tag belongs to information or a luggage/object label. Shape must follow content.

## Independent-meaning test

For every proposed fragment, complete this sentence: “This sticker records ___.” Keep it only when the blank names specific information.

Good answers:

- the complete Sydney Opera House silhouette;
- a person embroidering cloth;
- a complete silver neck ring together with the face and costume context;
- the upward view through a forest canopy;
- the creek's winding route through mossy stones.

Bad answers:

- three white roof arcs with no building context;
- a hat removed from an otherwise ordinary portrait;
- a hand with no legible action;
- a half-visible landmark inside a postage-stamp shape;
- an arbitrary piece of clothing.

## 20 portrait-detail studies to review

Use these as composition categories, not as mandatory outputs.

1. Eyes and expression, with enough brow and cheek context.
2. Full head-and-shoulders portrait.
3. Profile including the complete head and neck line.
4. Face plus distinctive earring.
5. Face plus complete necklace or neck ring.
6. Face framed by headwear, keeping the whole headwear silhouette.
7. Hands actively sewing, weaving, carving, painting, or playing.
8. Hands holding a tool together with the material being worked.
9. Hands adjusting a meaningful garment or accessory.
10. Hands touching another person, when relationship is the story.
11. Sleeve, hand, and craft surface as one action triangle.
12. Complete footwear plus stance when movement or identity is legible.
13. Full garment panel showing a complete motif repeat.
14. Jewelry cluster with its placement on the body still clear.
15. Back-of-head and shoulder only when hairstyle or garment construction is the subject.
16. Silhouette of the whole figure.
17. Reflection or mirror portrait that preserves facial identity.
18. Over-the-shoulder environmental portrait with task context.
19. Face and object interaction, such as camera, instrument, flower, or tool.
20. Paired details: expression plus corresponding action, displayed as two related frames.

Avoid crops through eyes, mouth, neck, wrists, elbows, knees, or ankles unless the framing is an unmistakably intentional extreme close-up. Crop above or below joints. Do not remove the head merely to feature jewelry.

## 10 minimal white-edge systems

Choose the lightest edge that separates the photograph from its ground. Keep every system simple; the contour belongs to the subject, not to decoration.

1. Clean contour — even 2–4 mm film-white outline following the subject closely.
2. Hand-scissor contour — slightly uneven 3–5 mm outline with long, calm changes rather than many small waves.
3. Micro torn-paper edge — restrained fibrous edge visible only at close range.
4. Soft deckle — a quiet feathered paper edge with no large teeth or curls.
5. Warm film-white — clean contour in aged ivory (`#F2EEE3`–`#E8E0D1`).
6. Cool film-white — clean contour in neutral photographic white (`#F3F3EF`–`#E8E9E5`).
7. Paper halo — narrow opaque edge plus a very soft translucent outer halo for presentation previews only.
8. Offset paper edge — clean edge with a 1–2 mm directional offset that suggests a hand-cut layer; use one direction only.
9. Double micro-keyline — film-white edge plus one hairline in a source-derived color; never add a thick second border.
10. Partial/open edge — white edge appears only where contrast requires it and fades to borderless elsewhere.

Avoid identical borders across the whole sheet. A border may be thin, thick, softly torn, film-like, or tag-shaped. Preserve useful label and luggage-tag structures; do not remove them merely to make the set more minimal. Do not add scallops, repeated bumps, ornamental corners, or dense unrelated waves. The arch-window sticker is a special case: keep the arch silhouette itself and add one white contour only; never place the arch inside an additional rectangle.

## 10 minimal film-frame systems

Choose a real frame logic and preserve the subject inside it.

1. Full-frame 35 mm rebate — thin black border, two quiet perforated edges, one frame number, complete image.
2. Minimal 35 mm rebate — one perforated edge and sparse edge code.
3. 120 square contact frame — clean black live edge, no sprocket holes.
4. 6×7 medium-format frame — wide image with quiet rebate and frame number.
5. Filed negative-carrier edge — irregular brushed black keyline around the complete negative.
6. Ragged darkroom border — soft chemical edge, no decorative sprockets.
7. Contact-sheet cell — one complete frame inside a larger indexed grid.
8. Slide mount — warm-white card mount with a square or landscape aperture.
9. Super 8 frame — small vertical frame with compact perforation rhythm.
10. Half-frame pair — two related complete vertical views in one 35 mm span.

Do not use perforations as generic decoration. Film borders communicate format and full-frame integrity. The image area must be composed independently before adding the border. Keep codes to one frame number or one short edge mark. Do not combine sprockets, torn paper, ornamental corners, several codes, and tape in one frame.

## Photo-to-paper edge transitions: 24-method reserve

Use this reserve for postcard image echoes, photo-to-sketch transitions, collage fragments, and collectible art fronts. These are selection methods, not filters to stack. First identify the protected subject, meaningful structural lines, native material, direction of movement, and intended spatial relationship. Select one primary method and, only when needed, one quiet supporting method.

1. **Directional multi-stop feather** — broad falloff with several unequal opacity stops; follow image flow rather than a centered vignette.
2. **Subject-contour dissolve** — fade around the real silhouette while keeping the subject intact.
3. **Structure-following line continuation** — extend roof, column, horizon, branch, seam, or object construction as pencil/ink beyond the photo.
4. **Luminance-threshold dissolve** — let highlights or shadows release first when light structure justifies it.
5. **Paper-grain displacement** — use local paper fibers to break alpha at micro scale after the broad transition is established.
6. **Dry-brush drag** — a few directional bristle gaps echo movement or painted surface; avoid an all-around brush frame.
7. **Watercolor bloom** — irregular pooled soft edge for water, sky, foliage, or atmospheric memories; preserve hard subject boundaries.
8. **Ink-wash bleed** — controlled dark-to-clear capillary edge for line-led or monochrome archive work.
9. **Graphite hatch release** — photo density gives way to sparse directional hatching aligned to form.
10. **Soft deckle edge** — restrained fibrous paper contour for a physical inserted print.
11. **Torn-fiber reveal** — a deliberate paper tear exposes or conceals one layer; use one meaningful tear, not decorative damage.
12. **Vellum diffusion edge** — a milky translucent sheet softens secondary detail without erasing its geometry.
13. **Acetate refraction edge** — a crisp clear boundary with tiny highlight/refraction for glass, museum, rain, or reflection logic.
14. **Transparent-film lift** — slight lifted corner, restrained crease, or air pocket creates depth and memory interruption.
15. **Tape-masked edge** — a hard/soft boundary exists because a sourced strip attaches or masks the layer; the strip must perform a task.
16. **Film-burn or light-leak release** — a narrow exposure transition for night, performance, or film-memory sources; never a universal warm glow.
17. **Emulsion-peel edge** — restrained Polaroid/darkroom chemical breakup suited to intimate analog memories.
18. **Stitched or fabric seam** — a woven/stitch line connects photographic and paper fields when textile evidence exists.
19. **Contact-sheet rebate** — a precise live frame edge, frame number, or carrier mark keeps the full photo legible.
20. **Blueprint registration edge** — construction lines, crop marks, or measured axes continue an architectural photo into paper.
21. **Atmospheric haze release** — mist, spray, smoke, or shallow depth already present in the source carries the transition.
22. **Layered cut-paper occlusion** — one physical paper layer hides part of a secondary image while protecting the main subject.
23. **Negative-space silhouette cut** — the real subject contour becomes the boundary between photo and open paper.
24. **Double-exposure bridge** — a low-opacity second crop connects regions through shared shape or light; never duplicate a face or landmark without purpose.

### Pairing guidance

- Landscape/water/forest: 1, 5, 7, 10, 21, or 23; keep horizons, ridges, streams, and major branches meaningful.
- Architecture/art space: 3, 9, 13, 19, or 20; preserve the complete landmark or interior structure.
- Portrait: 2, 5, 10, 12, 18, or 23; preserve the complete head, face, hands of narrative value, and body contour.
- Music/object: 3, 6, 13, 16, 17, 19, or 24; follow grooves, strings, cables, reflections, or object geometry.
- Postcard backs: favor 1, 3, 5, 9, 12, 19, 20, or 21 because writing and postal functions must stay crisp.

### Transition construction and rejection

Build a natural soft transition with at least three scales: a broad directional or subject-shaped alpha field, an irregular mid-scale mask, and restrained local paper/material breakup. Match the transition to real scene structure. Do not use a single centered oval, uniformly feathered rectangle, identical blur on all four sides, or an edge that cuts a protected subject. Inspect at both full-card scale and 100% crop; repair halos, muddy gray rims, abrupt opacity steps, repeated noise, accidental geometry, and fake distress.

## Photo-element abstraction: 20-method reserve

Use this reserve when a postcard back, sticker, or archive layout needs visual evidence from the source without repeating the complete photograph. Abstraction must preserve identifiable information rather than invent a generic shape. Before keeping an element, name what it records and why that information is independent.

1. **Complete silhouette** — reduce a landmark, person, instrument, plant, or object to its intact outer contour.
2. **Contour trace** — keep a few defining lines such as hair flow, shoulder line, roof curve, tonearm, or shoreline.
3. **Structural skeleton** — extract column grids, ceiling bays, floor perspective, trusses, façade axes, or object construction.
4. **Signature detail** — isolate one complete distinctive feature: capital, window, stylus, dial, clasp, or ornament.
5. **Material swatch** — sample characteristic textile, stone, wood, water, metal, glass, or paper without retaining the whole scene.
6. **Pattern repeat** — rebuild a visible clothing, tile, grille, groove, or façade rhythm as a restrained repeat.
7. **Source color field** — turn one observed light or material color into a simple block tied to a real feature.
8. **Light-and-shadow mass** — preserve a meaningful shadow, reflection, beam, or glow as an abstract tonal shape.
9. **Negative-space shape** — use the space between real subjects, such as sky between roof wings or water around a figure.
10. **Movement path** — trace gaze, gesture, water flow, road direction, tonearm travel, cable, seam, or other real motion.
11. **Reflection or refraction trace** — abstract highlights and distortions from glass, water, polished metal, or acetate.
12. **Depth layers** — separate foreground, middle ground, and background into two or three source-true translucent planes.
13. **Topographic or horizon profile** — reduce ridge, coast, skyline, canopy, or waterline to a continuous profile.
14. **Section or exploded logic** — separate meaningful object components according to real assembly, never arbitrary fragments.
15. **Proportional geometry** — translate real ratios, circles, arches, grids, or perspective angles into minimal geometry.
16. **Readable-source typographic fragment** — use only exact visible signage, numbering, label, or supplied wording as a graphic element.
17. **Circular rhythm** — derive concentric grooves, lens rings, wheels, records, lights, or round architectural features.
18. **Atmospheric field** — abstract mist, rain, grain, cloud, water shimmer, or light falloff without turning it into generic noise.
19. **Trace-plus-material pairing** — pair one contour or structural trace with one characteristic material or color sample.
20. **Detail constellation** — arrange two or three different meaningful details that describe separate facts; never use two crops of the same full image.

### Subject pairing

- Portrait: 1, 2, 5, 6, 8, 10, 11, 13, 18, or 19. Do not use a lower-body or clothing crop unless it records a complete garment, action, or distinctive motif.
- Architecture/art space: 1, 3, 4, 7, 9, 13, 15, 19, or 20. Prefer structure, ornament, and complete silhouette over duplicate façade thumbnails.
- Music/object: 2, 4, 5, 7, 8, 10, 11, 14, 15, 17, 19, or 20. A record groove, stylus path, lamp glow, or control geometry is more informative than a repeated turntable photo.
- Landscape: 5, 7, 8, 9, 10, 11, 12, 13, 18, or 19. Preserve actual horizon and flow direction.

### Mandatory rejection

Reject duplicated complete-image thumbnails, the same crop at two sizes, meaningless lower-edge strips, amputated body fragments, isolated clothing without a complete motif, unrecognizable architectural fragments, arbitrary decorative geometry, and any abstraction that could be swapped onto another photograph unchanged.

For creek and river fragments, trace the real visible water course from its source-side entry to its true visible endpoint in the photograph. Preserve enough surrounding stones to make both ends and the direction of flow legible. Use the generous winding silhouette demonstrated by the first rainforest study; do not shorten the water merely to reduce curves. Simplify only tiny serrations that do not describe the actual stream.

For portraits, never use a heavy black rectangle tight around an isolated head or face unless the user explicitly requests a mourning or archival-ID treatment. Default to borderless photography, warm/cool film-white, a quiet medium-format live edge with visible context, or a source-derived color keyline. Preserve the complete head.

## Terms used by this skill

- Mixed sticker edges: some stickers are borderless photographic cutouts and some use an irregular film-white paper edge. This does not mean mixing window shapes.
- Open postcard back: the message, address, stamp, and decorative echo are placed as separate functional zones without a mandatory center divider. “Open” describes the layout, not an unfinished design.
